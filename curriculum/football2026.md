## Phase 1 — Match-day ready

### Lesson 1: The beautiful game

#### What is football?

Football is the most popular sport on Earth. Around 3.5 billion people watch it. It is played in every country, in every climate, on grass, gravel, sand, and rooftops. The reason it travelled so far and grew so fast is the same reason it is called the beautiful game: at its most basic, all you need is a round object and two feet.

The version played at the World Cup is association football — the same rules in a park in Lisbon as in a stadium in Los Angeles.

---

#### The pitch

A football pitch is a large rectangle of grass (or artificial turf). The exact size can vary slightly, but a standard pitch is roughly 105 metres long and 68 metres wide — about the size of twelve tennis courts placed side by side.

Key areas to know:

- **The centre circle** — where every match kicks off, and where kick-offs restart after a goal.
- **The penalty area** (also called "the box") — the large rectangle in front of each goal. A foul by a defender inside this box gives the attacking team a penalty kick.
- **The six-yard box** — a smaller rectangle right in front of the goal. Goal kicks are taken from inside it.
- **The goal** — 7.32 metres wide and 2.44 metres high. The crossbar is the horizontal bar at the top; the posts are the two vertical bars at the sides.

---

#### The two teams

Each team has 11 players on the pitch at once. A full squad for a major tournament like the World Cup is 26 players, but only 11 start and a maximum of 3 (or sometimes 5) can be substituted during a match.

The four main positions:

**Goalkeeper (GK)** — the only player allowed to use their hands, but only inside their own penalty area. Their job is to stop the ball going into the net. The goalkeeper wears a different coloured shirt so referees can tell them apart.

**Defenders** — their main job is to stop the other team scoring. Usually 3 or 4 of them. Centre-backs defend the middle. Full-backs defend the sides (left-back and right-back). Defenders often carry the ball out from the back to start attacks.

**Midfielders** — the engine room of the team. They work between defence and attack. A defensive midfielder (sometimes called a "six") shields the back four. A central midfielder (the "eight") gets the ball and keeps play moving. An attacking midfielder (the "ten") is usually the most creative player — someone who makes things happen.

**Forwards** — their job is to score goals. A striker (the "nine") leads the attack. Wingers play out wide and try to cross the ball or cut inside. France often play with one central striker and two wide forwards, one of whom is Kylian Mbappé.

---

#### How a match works

A match lasts **90 minutes**, split into two halves of 45 minutes each with a half-time break (usually 15 minutes). The referee can add **injury time** (also called "stoppage time") at the end of each half — usually 2 to 10 minutes — to make up for time lost to substitutions, injuries, and VAR reviews.

The team that scores more goals wins. Simple.

If the score is level at full time during a knock-out match (not the group stage), the game goes to **extra time** — two more periods of 15 minutes. If it is still level after that, the match is decided by a **penalty shootout**.

---

#### Scoring a goal

A goal is scored when the entire ball crosses the entire goal line, between the posts and under the crossbar. Even if the ball goes in and bounces back out, as long as it fully crossed the line, it counts. Goal-line technology — a system of cameras and sensors — confirms this instantly.

A player can score with any part of their body except their hand or arm (unless it is accidental and the referee judges the position to be natural).

---

#### France connection

France typically line up in a **4-3-3** formation — four defenders, three midfielders, three forwards. Mbappé plays on the left side of the front three, but he drifts inside constantly, which causes huge problems for defenders. When you watch Les Bleus, notice how the whole team seems to wait for the moment they can release him into space.

### Lesson 2: Rules you must know

The rules of football are officially called the **Laws of the Game** and are maintained by a body called IFAB (International Football Association Board). There are 17 laws. You do not need to know all of them. Here are the ones that come up every match.

---

#### Offside — the big one

Offside is the most debated call in football. Here is the basic idea:

A player is in an offside **position** if they are in the opponent's half AND nearer to the opponent's goal line than both the ball and the second-to-last defender (the last defender is usually the goalkeeper).

Being in an offside position is not automatically a foul. A player is only penalised for offside if they are in that position **at the moment the ball is played to them**, AND they are involved in active play (touching the ball, interfering with a defender, etc.).

**Simple example:** France are attacking. Mbappé is behind the last England defender when a French midfielder plays the ball forward to him. Mbappé is offside — the pass is disallowed.

**The grey area:** the rule uses "any part of the body that can legally score a goal." This means a toe, a shoulder, or even a strand of hair can make a player offside, even if the rest of the body is onside. VAR (see below) is used to draw lines on freeze-frames of the exact moment the ball is kicked. This is why you sometimes see a 30-second wait before a goal is confirmed.

---

#### Fouls

A foul is an unfair physical challenge or deliberate handball. Common fouls:

- Tripping an opponent
- Pushing with the hand or arm
- Jumping into an opponent carelessly
- Sliding through the ball and making contact with the player first (a dangerous tackle)
- Deliberately handling the ball (handball)

When a foul occurs, the opposing team gets a **free kick** from the spot where the foul happened.

- A **direct free kick** can be shot directly at goal.
- An **indirect free kick** must touch another player before a goal can be scored (rare, mainly for technical fouls).

If a foul happens inside the penalty box, the opposing team gets a **penalty kick** instead (see below).

---

#### Set pieces

These are any situation where play restarts after being stopped:

- **Corner kick** — when the ball goes out over the goal line and was last touched by a defender. The attacking team kicks from the corner arc. Most goals from corners come from headers.
- **Throw-in** — when the ball goes out over the touchline (the long sides of the pitch), the opposing team throws it back in with both hands, from behind the head, with both feet on the ground.
- **Goal kick** — when the attacking team last touches the ball before it goes out over the goal line, the defending team gets a goal kick from the six-yard box.

---

#### Yellow and red cards

Referees use cards to discipline players:

- **Yellow card** (a "booking") — a warning for serious fouls, time-wasting, unsporting behaviour, or persistent fouling. If a player receives two yellow cards in the same game, they are immediately shown a red card and sent off. In tournaments, a player who accumulates two yellows in separate games is suspended for the next match.

- **Red card** — shown for violent conduct, deliberate handball to stop a clear goal-scoring chance, serious foul play, or two yellows. The player must leave the pitch immediately and their team continues with 10 players. The player is automatically banned for the next match at minimum.

---

#### VAR — Video Assistant Referee

VAR is a system introduced in 2018 that allows a team of video referees in a review centre to check four types of decisions:

1. Goals (and the build-up to them — fouls, handball, offside)
2. Penalty decisions
3. Red card incidents
4. Cases of mistaken identity

The on-pitch referee can also walk over to a pitch-side monitor to review an incident themselves, called an **on-field review (OFR)**. When VAR is active, you will see the referee touch their ear and look upward — they are hearing from the review team.

VAR is controversial because it slows the game down and its application can feel inconsistent. But it has removed many clear, match-changing errors.

---

#### Penalties and shootouts

A penalty kick is taken from the penalty spot — 11 metres from the goal. Only the goalkeeper and the taker are inside the penalty area. All other players must wait outside.

The goalkeeper must stay on the goal line until the ball is kicked (though in practice they usually creep forward slightly). They can move sideways as much as they want. A penalty is a high-pressure, one-on-one moment: statistically, the taker scores about 75% of the time at the highest level.

A **penalty shootout** (used to settle knock-out matches after extra time) works like this:

- Each team picks five players to take a penalty
- Teams alternate kicks (Team A, then Team B, then Team A...)
- If the score is still level after five each, the shootout goes to sudden death — each team takes one penalty at a time, and the first to miss while the other scores is eliminated
- The goalkeeper from the shootout does not have to be the same goalkeeper who played the match

France are infamous for losing important penalty shootouts. They went out of Euro 2020 on penalties against Switzerland. In 2022 they lost on penalties to Argentina in the final — after Mbappé had scored a hat trick to take the game to 3-3.

### Lesson 3: The World Cup explained

#### What it is

The FIFA World Cup is the biggest sporting event on Earth. It happens every four years. More people watch the World Cup final than any other event in sport — the 2022 final between Argentina and France was watched by an estimated 1.5 billion people.

FIFA — Fédération Internationale de Football Association — is the governing body that organises it. They have 211 member associations, which is more than the United Nations has member states.

---

#### A brief history

The first World Cup was held in **1930 in Uruguay**. Uruguay hosted and won it. Only 13 teams competed — transport was so difficult that several European nations did not bother to make the six-week boat crossing.

Since then, the tournament has only been cancelled twice: 1942 and 1946, because of World War II.

Key moments in World Cup history:

- **1950** — the "Maracanazo." Brazil hosted the tournament and were expected to win it easily. In the deciding match at the Maracanã stadium — in front of nearly 200,000 people — Uruguay beat Brazil 2-1. It remains one of the greatest upsets in sporting history. Brazil still consider it a national trauma.
- **1966** — England's only World Cup win, on home soil.
- **1970** — widely considered the greatest World Cup ever. Brazil, with Pelé, played some of the most beautiful football the game has ever seen and won 4-1 in the final.
- **1986** — Maradona's tournament. More in Lesson 10.
- **1998** — France's first World Cup, on home soil. More in Lesson 4.
- **2010** — the first World Cup held in Africa (South Africa). The vuvuzela horn became its soundtrack.
- **2014** — Germany beat Argentina 1-0 in the final. Germany had also beaten Brazil 7-1 in the semi-final — one of the most shocking results in football history.
- **2022** — Argentina beat France in what many consider the greatest World Cup final ever played.

---

#### How it works — qualification

From the moment one World Cup ends, teams around the world start trying to qualify for the next one. Every country in the world (that is a FIFA member) plays qualifying matches in their **continental zone**:

- Europe (UEFA): 55 nations compete in groups, top teams qualify
- South America (CONMEBOL): all 10 nations play each other twice in a league, top 6 qualify directly
- Africa, Asia, North/Central America, and Oceania each have their own systems

Qualifying takes roughly two years. Getting knocked out of qualifying — failing to reach the World Cup at all — is a national disaster in most countries. Italy, one of football's great nations, failed to qualify for both 2018 and 2022.

---

#### The 2026 format

The 2026 World Cup has **expanded to 48 teams** (up from 32 in previous tournaments). This is the first time this new format is used.

The structure:

1. **Group stage** — 12 groups of 4 teams each. Each team plays the other three teams in their group once. A win = 3 points, a draw = 1 point, a loss = 0 points. The top two teams from each group advance, plus eight of the best third-placed teams — making 32 teams in total for the knockout stage.

2. **Round of 32** — the 32 remaining teams play single-leg knockout matches. Lose and you go home.

3. **Round of 16** — 16 teams remain. Same rules.

4. **Quarter-finals** — 8 teams.

5. **Semi-finals** — 4 teams.

6. **Third-place play-off** — the two losing semi-finalists play for bronze.

7. **The final** — the last two teams left standing. If level after 90 minutes, extra time and then penalties.

---

#### 2026 host nations

The 2026 World Cup is hosted across three countries — **the United States, Canada, and Mexico**. This is the first time three nations have co-hosted the World Cup.

16 stadiums are being used across the three countries. The final will be held at MetLife Stadium in New Jersey (the New York metropolitan area), which holds around 82,500 spectators.

Mexico is the first country to host World Cup matches in three separate tournaments (1970, 1986, 2026). The USA hosted in 1994. Canada is hosting for the first time.

---

#### Records to know

- **Most World Cup wins:** Brazil with 5 (1958, 1962, 1970, 1994, 2002)
- **Most finals reached:** Germany with 8 (won 4, lost 4)
- **Most goals in a single tournament:** Just Fontaine of France — 13 goals in 1958. This record has stood for nearly 70 years.
- **Most goals in World Cup history:** Miroslav Klose of Germany — 16 goals across four tournaments
- **Fastest goal:** Hakan Şükür of Turkey scored after just 11 seconds against South Korea in 2002
- **Biggest win:** Hungary 10-1 El Salvador, 1982

## Phase 2 — France and the stars

---

### Lesson 4: Les Bleus — your team

#### The name

France's national team are called **Les Bleus** — The Blues — because of their traditional royal blue shirts. The official name is the Équipe de France de football. They play their home matches at the **Stade de France** in Saint-Denis, just north of Paris, which holds about 81,000 people.

---

#### Early history

France were one of the founding nations of FIFA and one of only 13 teams at the first World Cup in 1930. However, for most of the 20th century they were an inconsistent force — occasionally brilliant, often disappointing.

Their first taste of glory came at **Euro 1984**, hosted in France. Michel Platini — arguably the greatest French player before Zidane — scored 9 goals in 5 games, including a hat-trick in a single match. France won the tournament. Platini later became the president of UEFA (European football's governing body), though his career ended in controversy over financial irregularities.

---

#### 1998 — the first World Cup

This is where it really began. France hosted the 1998 World Cup and, after years of near misses, finally won it.

The team was built on an extraordinary generation: Zinedine Zidane in midfield, Lilian Thuram and Marcel Desailly at the back, Thierry Henry and David Trezeguet up front. Coach Aimé Jacquet was criticised throughout the tournament for not playing his best attackers enough — the French media were brutal — but he kept his nerve.

In the final against Brazil at the Stade de France, Zidane scored two headers from corners in the first half. France won **3-0**. The whole country poured into the streets. An estimated one million people gathered on the Champs-Élysées in Paris.

The team was also celebrated as a symbol of modern, multicultural France. The phrase **"Black, Blanc, Beur"** (Black, White, North African) was coined to describe the team's diversity, reflecting France's multicultural identity.

---

#### 2000 — back to back

Two years later, France won **Euro 2000** in Belgium and the Netherlands, becoming the first team to hold the World Cup and European Championship simultaneously. They beat Italy in the final with a golden goal in extra time from David Trezeguet.

It remains the peak of French football.

---

#### 2002 and the fall

France went to the 2002 World Cup in Japan and South Korea as hot favourites and defending champions. They were eliminated in the group stage without scoring a single goal. Zidane was injured and barely played. It was a humiliation.

---

#### 2006 — Zidane's last dance

Zidane came out of international retirement for the 2006 World Cup in Germany. France, written off before the tournament, made it all the way to the final. In the final against Italy, Zidane opened the scoring with a cheeky Panenka penalty (a soft chip down the middle of the goal while the goalkeeper dives). Then, in extra time, he was provoked by Italian defender Marco Materazzi — who insulted his family — and responded by headbutting him in the chest. Zidane was shown a red card and walked past the World Cup trophy on his way off the pitch. Italy won on penalties.

The headbutt remains one of the most talked-about moments in football history. Zidane has never fully explained what was said.

---

#### 2018 — champions again

France won their second World Cup at the 2018 tournament in Russia, beating Croatia **4-2** in the final. The team was coached by Didier Deschamps — the same player who captained the 1998 winning side, now the manager.

Key to the win was a 19-year-old named **Kylian Mbappé**, who became only the second teenager ever to score in a World Cup final (after Pelé in 1958). Mbappé was electric throughout the tournament — fast, direct, impossible to catch. The world took notice.

---

#### 2022 — heartbreak

The 2022 World Cup final in Qatar between Argentina and France is widely considered one of the greatest finals ever played.

Argentina led 2-0 with 10 minutes left. Then Mbappé scored — penalty. Then, 97 seconds later, Mbappé scored again — a stunning volley. 2-2. Extra time. Mbappé scored again to make it 3-3 — completing a hat-trick. In the end, Argentina won on penalties. Mbappé finished with 8 goals in the tournament. He lost, but he was extraordinary.

---

#### Current playing style

France under Deschamps tend to play with a **solid defensive shape** and use the pace of their forwards — especially Mbappé — to attack quickly on the counter. They do not always play the most beautiful football, but they are incredibly hard to beat and lethal when they score. Think: well-organised at the back, devastating on the break.

### Lesson 5: Stars to watch

#### Kylian Mbappé — France

Born in 1998 in Bondy, a suburb northeast of Paris. His father is Cameroonian, his mother Algerian-Kabyle. He grew up supporting Real Madrid and dreaming of playing for France.

He joined Paris Saint-Germain's academy at 14, made his professional debut at 16, and by 19 had won the World Cup. He moved to **Real Madrid** in 2024, fulfilling a childhood dream.

By the time of the 2026 World Cup, Mbappé is 27 — at the absolute peak of his career. He is arguably the best player on the planet. What makes him special: pure speed (one of the fastest players ever), ice-cold finishing, and the ability to perform in the biggest moments. He already has more World Cup final goals than any other player in history.

---

#### Other key French players

**Marcus Thuram** — striker, son of legendary French defender Lilian Thuram. Powerful, good in the air, useful when France need to hold the ball up. Plays for Inter Milan in Italy.

**Aurélien Tchouaméni** — defensive midfielder who plays for Real Madrid alongside Mbappé. Excellent at winning the ball back and driving forward. A quiet, composed presence.

**Eduardo Camavinga** — also Real Madrid. Left-footed, energetic, technically brilliant. Plays as a midfielder and is one of the most exciting young players in European club football.

**William Saliba** — centre-back, Arsenal. One of the best defenders of his generation. Calm under pressure, good with the ball at his feet — important for a team that likes to build from the back.

**Randal Kolo Muani** — forward, Paris Saint-Germain. Fast, direct, hard-working. A useful option off the bench.

#### Global stars to know

**Vinicius Jr (Brazil)** — plays for Real Madrid. One of the most exciting wingers in the world. Explosive pace, slick dribbling, and he thrives on the biggest stages. Brazilian football has always been associated with flair and joy, and Vinicius carries that tradition.

**Jude Bellingham (England)** — plays for Real Madrid. Signed for over €100 million in 2023. Enormous energy, box-to-box midfielder, strong, technically excellent. Captain material for England. Born in 2003, he is still only 22 at this World Cup.

**Lamine Yamal (Spain)** — born in 2007. Not a typo. He was 16 years old when he played in Euro 2024, the youngest player ever in a European Championship final, and he won it. By the 2026 World Cup he is still only 18. He plays for Barcelona and is an exceptional natural talent — quick, left-footed, loves taking people on.

**Pedri (Spain)** — Barcelona's creative midfielder. Technically immaculate. He sees passes that other players do not even imagine. Spain's most important player in midfield.

**Rodri (Spain)** — Real Madrid. A deep-lying midfielder who controls the tempo of games. Won the Ballon d'Or in 2024. Often described as the best midfielder in the world.

**Erling Haaland (Norway)** — Manchester City's striker. Extraordinary goal-scorer — he broke all kinds of Premier League records in his first season. His weakness has historically been international football: Norway failed to qualify for the 2026 World Cup, so Haaland is not there. But you will hear his name constantly.

### Lesson 6: Know the competition

#### Brazil

**Nickname:** A Seleção (The Selection) or Canarinha (Little Canary, after the yellow shirt)
**World Cup wins:** 5 — more than any other nation
**Style:** Brazil have always been associated with flair, creativity, and attacking brilliance. The greatest players in history — Pelé, Ronaldo, Ronaldinho, Neymar — all wore yellow and blue. **To watch for:** Vinicius Jr, Rodrygo, Raphinha.

#### Argentina

**Nickname:** La Albiceleste (The White and Sky Blues)
**World Cup wins:** 3 (1978, 1986, 2022)
**Style:** Passionate, combative, technically excellent. Argentina have always had a culture of producing brilliant number tens — creative, attacking midfielders. Their greatest ever player, Lionel Messi, led them to the 2022 title.

---

#### Spain

**Nickname:** La Roja (The Red)

The current generation plays with more directness and pace, but the technical quality remains extraordinary.

#### England

**Nickname:** The Three Lions
**World Cup wins:** 1 (1966, on home soil)

The phrase "Football's coming home" — from the 1996 song Three Lions — is sung hopefully every tournament, and every tournament something goes wrong.
**Style:** Physical, direct, hard-working. English football is associated with pace and power rather than technical finesse, though the current generation is much more technically developed.
**To watch for:** Jude Bellingham, Harry Kane (Bayern Munich's prolific striker), Bukayo Saka (Arsenal).

---

#### Germany

**Nickname:** Die Mannschaft (The Team)
**World Cup wins:** 4 (1954, 1974, 1990, 2014)
**Style:** Germany are the most consistent nation in World Cup history. They have reached 8 finals, won 4, never finished worse than third until 2018 (when they were eliminated in the group stage for the first time since 1938 — a huge national shock). Known for being organised, well-drilled, hard to beat, and for producing entire systems of excellent footballers rather than relying on individual brilliance.
**To watch for:** Florian Wirtz (Bayer Leverkusen), Jamal Musiala (Bayern Munich) — two extraordinary young players.

#### Portugal

**Nickname:** A Seleção das Quinas (The Team of the Shields)
**World Cup wins:** 0 (best result: 3rd in 1966)

**Current generation:** Bruno Fernandes (Manchester United), Rúben Neves, Rafael Leão — Portugal have plenty of talent beyond Ronaldo.

### Lesson 7: Reading a match

#### Formations

A formation describes how the ten outfield players (everyone except the goalkeeper) are arranged on the pitch. It is written as three numbers separated by dashes, from back to front.

**4-3-3** — Four defenders, three midfielders, three forwards. France's typical formation. Common across Europe. Gives width in attack through the two wide forwards.

**4-4-2** — The classic formation of English football. Four defenders, four midfielders, two strikers side by side. Less common at the top level now, but some coaches still use it.

**4-2-3-1** — Four defenders, two holding midfielders sitting deep, three attacking midfielders, one striker. A very solid, structured shape.

**3-5-2** — Three centre-backs, five midfielders (including two wing-backs who cover the full width of the pitch), and two forwards. Used by teams that want to dominate possession in midfield.

**4-3-2-1** — Sometimes called the Christmas tree. Four defenders, three midfielders, two attacking midfielders, one striker. Compact, hard to break down.

Formations are starting points, not rigid rules. During a game, players constantly shift, overlap, and cover for each other. The shape at kick-off rarely stays exactly the same throughout 90 minutes.

---

#### Key tactical concepts

**High press** — when a team aggressively presses the opposition high up the pitch (in their own half or even their defensive third). The aim is to force errors and win the ball close to the opponent's goal. Liverpool and Manchester City are famous for this. It requires enormous energy and fitness.

**Counter-attack** — when a team defends deep and then attacks rapidly the moment they win the ball. Often used by teams facing stronger opponents. The idea is to absorb pressure, then release fast forwards into the space behind a high defensive line. France under Deschamps do this brilliantly.

**High defensive line** — when a team's defence pushes up the pitch, staying close to the halfway line. This compresses the space in midfield and puts strikers in an offside trap. The risk: if a striker gets in behind, there is a lot of open space to run into.

**Possession football** — prioritising keeping the ball rather than playing direct. Spain mastered this. The idea is that you cannot concede if you have the ball.

**Pressing trap** — setting up the pitch so that the opposition is encouraged to pass the ball to a particular area, then pouncing with several players at once to win it back.

---

#### Commentator phrases decoded

- **"He's in behind"** — a forward has got in behind the defensive line, into the space between the last defender and the goalkeeper.
- **"Over the top"** — a long ball played over the defence into that space.
- **"Playing out from the back"** — the goalkeeper and defenders passing the ball rather than hoofing it forward.
- **"Parking the bus"** — extremely defensive, deep-lying tactics. (The phrase was reportedly coined by José Mourinho.)
- **"Clinical"** — efficient finishing, not wasting chances.
- **"All day long"** — very easy. "He should score that all day long."
- **"Bottles it"** — fails under pressure (usually in a penalty shootout context).
- **"xG"** (expected goals) — a statistical measure of how likely any given shot is to result in a goal, based on where the shot came from, what part of the body was used, and what type of chance it was. A shot from six yards out in the centre of the goal has a high xG (maybe 0.7 — meaning it is expected to be scored 7 out of 10 times). A long-range speculative effort has a very low xG (maybe 0.03). When pundits say "they underperformed their xG," they mean the team created better chances than the scoreline suggests.
- **"PPDA"** — passes allowed per defensive action, a measure of how aggressively a team presses.
- **"The gaffer"** — the manager.

### Lesson 8: Clubs and leagues

#### Why clubs matter

When football players are not playing for their national teams (which is only for a few weeks a year), they play for their clubs. Clubs are where players spend 95% of their careers. A player might represent their country 100 times across their whole career, but they will play 300-400 matches for their club or clubs.

Club football runs from August to May each year (with a mid-season break in some countries in winter). National team football fits into international windows — usually a week or two in September, October, November, and March.

---

#### The big five leagues

**Premier League — England**
The richest, most-watched domestic football league in the world. 20 teams, plays August to May. The global TV rights deal generates billions of pounds. Clubs include Manchester City, Arsenal, Liverpool, Chelsea, Manchester United, and Tottenham Hotspur. Known for pace, physicality, and relentless intensity. Almost every top player in the world has played or wants to play here.

**La Liga — Spain**
Home to two of the world's biggest clubs: Real Madrid and FC Barcelona. Historically dominated by those two, though Atletico Madrid have broken the duopoly occasionally. Technically refined, tactically intelligent football. Also home to clubs from the Basque Country (Athletic Club, Real Sociedad) with unique rules about only playing players from their region.

**Bundesliga — Germany**
The league with the highest average stadium attendance in the world. Bayern Munich have won it over 30 times and dominated German football for decades. Borussia Dortmund are their great rivals. German football is known for pace, energy, and excellent youth development.

**Serie A — Italy**
The Italian top flight. Home to Juventus, AC Milan, Inter Milan, AS Roma, Napoli, and Lazio. Serie A was the dominant league in the world through the 1980s and 1990s — when the best players all came to Italy. It has slightly fallen behind the Premier League and La Liga commercially, but Italian football remains tactically sophisticated and deeply passionate.

**Ligue 1 — France**
France's top division. Paris Saint-Germain have dominated it almost entirely since Qatari ownership took over in 2011 and brought in huge spending. Marseille (known as OM) are the most historically successful French club and PSG's greatest rivals, with a famously intense fanbase. Lyon have won the women's Champions League many times and are a force in women's football globally.

#### The Champions League

The **UEFA Champions League** is the most prestigious club competition in the world. Every year, the top clubs from each European league qualify to compete in a knockout tournament to find the best club in Europe.

The final is held at a neutral venue and is one of the most-watched sporting events globally. Famous finals: Liverpool vs AC Milan 2005 (Liverpool came back from 3-0 down at half-time to win on penalties); Real Madrid have won it 15 times, more than any other club.

Winning the Champions League alongside your domestic league is called the **Double**. Winning the league, the Champions League, and the domestic cup is called the **Treble** — very rare and very prestigious.

---

#### Promotion and relegation

This is one of football's most unique features. In almost every football league in the world, the bottom clubs are **relegated** — they drop down into a lower division the following season. And the top clubs from the lower division are **promoted** — they rise up.

In England, the bottom 3 teams in the Premier League are relegated to the Championship every season. The top 2 teams in the Championship come straight up, and the next four play off for the third promotion place.

This means that a small club from a small town can, with enough success, reach the top tier of football. And a famous club can fall. Sunderland, once a major English club, spent years in the third tier. Leeds United have been up and down. This gives every match real stakes, right to the end of the season.

American sports do not have this — a franchise in the NFL or NBA cannot be relegated. Many football fans find this fundamental to the sport's drama.

### Lesson 9: Transfers and the money

#### What is a transfer?

When a player moves from one club to another, it is called a **transfer**. The buying club pays the selling club a **transfer fee** in exchange for the rights to the player's contract. This fee can be anywhere from a few thousand euros for a lower-league player to hundreds of millions for a superstar.

The transfer fee is paid club-to-club. It is separate from the player's wage, which is paid weekly or monthly by the new club.

---

#### Transfer windows

Transfers can only happen during set periods called **transfer windows**:

- **Summer window** — roughly June to August or September (depending on the country). The busiest window, after the season ends and before the new one begins.
- **Winter window** — roughly January. A shorter window where clubs can buy and sell to react to injuries, poor form, or unexpected opportunities.

Outside of these windows, clubs cannot register new players to play in league competition (though they can sometimes agree deals in advance).

---

#### How a transfer works

1. Club A wants to sign a player from Club B.
2. Club A makes a bid to Club B (this is often done privately, through agents and directors).
3. Club B either accepts, rejects, or counter-offers.
4. Once clubs agree a fee, the player is given permission to speak with Club A.
5. The player (and their agent) negotiate personal terms — mainly the wage, contract length, and signing bonus.
6. If both sides agree, the player undergoes a medical examination.
7. The deal is completed and announced publicly.

The announcement is now a significant media event. Clubs put considerable effort into creative announcement videos and social media posts.

---

#### Agents

A football agent represents a player in negotiations. They find clubs, negotiate wages and fees, and take a commission — usually around 10% of the transfer fee or a percentage of the player's wages. Top agents are enormously powerful figures in the game. Kylian Mbappé's agent is his mother, Fayza Lamari, who is known for driving a very hard bargain.

---

#### Loan deals

A loan is a temporary transfer. Club A sends a player to Club B for 6 months or a season. Club B pays the player's wages (or part of them). At the end of the loan, the player returns to Club A.

Loans are used to give young players experience at a level appropriate to their development, or to find a player playing time if they are not getting it at their current club.

---

#### Free transfers

When a player's contract expires, they become a **free agent** — they can sign for any club without a transfer fee being paid. This is called a free transfer (or Bosman, after the 1995 European Court of Justice ruling that made it legal). Clubs save enormously on the fee, though the player often negotiates a higher wage in return.

---

#### Where does the money come from?

Modern top clubs are multi-hundred-million-euro businesses with several revenue streams:

- **Broadcasting rights** — TV deals, especially the Premier League, generate billions. Clubs receive a share based on their position and number of TV appearances.
- **Matchday** — ticket sales, hospitality, food and drink at the stadium.
- **Commercial** — shirt sponsorship, sleeve sponsorship, training kit deals, merchandise, stadium naming rights.
- **Prize money** — from competitions like the Champions League.

The richest clubs — Real Madrid, Manchester City, Barcelona, Manchester United — generate over €700 million per year. This is why they can pay €100 million transfer fees and €500,000-per-week wages.

---

#### Financial Fair Play

UEFA introduced Financial Fair Play (FFP) rules — now called Financial Sustainability Regulations (FSR) — to prevent clubs from spending wildly beyond their means and building up dangerous debts. The rules essentially require clubs to not spend significantly more than they earn.

However, the rules have been controversial and inconsistently applied. Manchester City were charged with breaking them over 100 times and faced a lengthy legal process. Several clubs find creative workarounds through commercial deals with sponsors connected to their owners.

### Lesson 10: Legends, records, and culture

#### The immortals

**Pelé (Brazil, 1940–2022)**

Edson Arantes do Nascimento — known to the world as Pelé — is the only player to have won three World Cups (1958, 1962, 1970). He made his World Cup debut at 17 years old, scoring in the final. He played his entire Brazilian club career at Santos, scoring over 600 goals in official matches (with claims of over 1,000 including friendlies).

Pelé was not just a great player. He was the first global football superstar — the first footballer whose name was known on every continent. He played in an era before satellite TV, and yet the whole world knew who he was.

He died in December 2022, aged 82. Brazil wore black armbands throughout the 2022 World Cup in his memory.

---

**Diego Maradona (Argentina, 1960–2020)**

Maradona is the most mythological figure in football history. Not necessarily the greatest player — though many argue he is — but the most dramatic, the most human, the most contradictory.

He led Argentina to victory in the **1986 World Cup** in Mexico, which is considered almost entirely his own achievement. In the quarter-final against England, he scored two of the most famous goals ever — in the same match:

- **The Hand of God** — a goal he punched in with his fist, which the referee did not see. He later said: "It was a little bit the hand of Maradona, and a little bit the hand of God."
- **The Goal of the Century** — voted the greatest goal in World Cup history. Maradona received the ball in his own half and dribbled past five England players and the goalkeeper in roughly 11 seconds, covering 60 metres of pitch.

He played his club career mainly at Barcelona and Napoli. At Napoli, he was worshipped like a religious figure — a poor southern Italian city whose team had never won the Italian league. Under Maradona, they won it twice. There are still murals of him all over Naples.

He died in November 2020, aged 60. Argentina's victory at the 2022 World Cup felt, to many fans, like completing the work he had started.

---

**Johan Cruyff (Netherlands, 1947–2016)**

The Dutch genius. Cruyff played for Ajax and Barcelona and is considered the architect of modern football. He invented — or at least codified — the concept of **Total Football**: a system where every player can play in any position, the team constantly shifts shape, and space is controlled collectively. Ajax won three consecutive European Cups in the early 1970s playing this way. The Netherlands, with Cruyff leading them, reached the 1974 World Cup final (losing to West Germany). That Dutch team is considered by many to be the greatest team never to win the World Cup.

His influence on football is immeasurable. The Barcelona style of play that produced the great teams of 2008-2015 is directly descended from Cruyff's ideas. He also invented the **Cruyff turn** — a simple but devastatingly effective feint used by players to this day.

---

**Zinedine Zidane (France, 1972–present)**

France's greatest player, born in Marseille to Algerian immigrant parents. Technically extraordinary — elegant, powerful, intelligent, with a rare ability to control a match. He was at his best when the game was at its biggest: the 1998 World Cup final, the 2002 Champions League final (when he scored one of the most beautiful goals ever seen, a left-foot volley from outside the box for Real Madrid against Bayer Leverkusen).

He won the World Cup in 1998 and Euro 2000 as a player. He retired after the 2006 headbutt incident. He then became a manager — and managed Real Madrid to three consecutive Champions League titles, something never done before or since. He retired as a manager in 2021.

If anyone in France says "Zizou," they mean Zidane.

---

**Ronaldo — R9 (Brazil, 1976–present)**

Not to be confused with Cristiano Ronaldo. This is Ronaldo Luís Nazário de Lima — known as R9 or "The Phenomenon." Widely considered the most complete striker who ever lived before injuries interrupted his career. Fast as a sprinter, technically like a winger, finishing like a killer.

He was the face of the **2002 World Cup** in Japan and South Korea. Brazil won it 5-0 in the final against Germany. Ronaldo scored both goals. He had been suffering from mysterious seizures and illness in the years leading up to it, and his 2002 performances felt like a return from the edge.

He once scored a goal from a free kick where he just ran up and blasted it top-corner with his right foot. No curve, no placement. Just power. It went in.

---

#### Other legends worth knowing

- **Lionel Messi (Argentina)** — 7 Ballon d'Or awards (the most ever). Won the 2022 World Cup. Many consider him the greatest of all time.
- **Cristiano Ronaldo (Portugal)** — 5 Ballon d'Ors. 900+ career goals. The most followed person on Instagram with over 600 million followers.
- **Ronaldinho (Brazil)** — played for Barcelona in the mid-2000s and was arguably the most entertaining footballer who ever lived. Watch highlights.
- **Thierry Henry (France)** — France's greatest striker. Record goalscorer for Arsenal. Silky, fast, deadly.
- **Lilian Thuram (France)** — defender who scored one of the most important goals in French football history (two goals in the 1998 semi-final, having scored only once before in his career).

#### Records and remarkable numbers

- **Most World Cup goals:** Miroslav Klose (Germany), 16 goals across 4 tournaments
- **Most goals in a single tournament:** Just Fontaine (France), 13 goals in 1958. This record has survived almost 70 years.
- **Fastest goal in World Cup history:** 11 seconds — Hakan Şükür for Turkey vs South Korea, 2002
- **Biggest World Cup victory:** Hungary 10-1 El Salvador, 1982
- **Youngest World Cup scorer:** Pelé, 17 years and 239 days, 1958
- **Most international appearances:** Cristiano Ronaldo, over 200 caps for Portugal
- **Most expensive goalkeeper ever:** Gianluigi Donnarumma moved from AC Milan to PSG for approximately €68 million

---

#### Interesting facts

- At the first World Cup in 1930, both teams used their own ball. Argentina brought one ball, Uruguay brought another. The compromise: Argentina's ball in the first half, Uruguay's in the second. Uruguay were winning 1-0 at half-time. They won 4-2.
- The 1950 World Cup had no final. The title was decided by a final group stage. Uruguay beat Brazil in the last match in front of nearly 200,000 people in Rio de Janeiro. The stadium fell completely silent. Some Brazilian fans cried for days.
- The offside rule was changed in 1925 because games were too low-scoring (defenders had learned to trap attackers offside too easily). One rule change immediately increased average goals per game by nearly 50%.
- The penalty shootout was introduced in the World Cup in 1978. England were knocked out of the World Cup on penalties in 1990, 1998, 2006, and 2020 (Euros). They finally won one in 2018 — against Colombia in the last 16.
- A goalkeeper has never scored at the World Cup from open play. Many have scored from penalties.

#### Football culture

**Derbies** — a match between two local rival clubs. Named after the English town of Derby, where a chaotic early form of football was played between two parishes in the street. Famous derbies:

- **El Clásico** — Real Madrid vs Barcelona (Spain's two most powerful clubs, ideologically and geographically opposed)
- **Derby della Madonnina** — AC Milan vs Inter Milan
- **Merseyside Derby** — Liverpool vs Everton
- **North London Derby** — Arsenal vs Tottenham Hotspur
- **Le Classique** — Paris Saint-Germain vs Marseille (France's fiercest rivalry)

**Club scarves** — a tradition going back over a century. Fans wear their club's scarf to matches, and sometimes hold them above their heads during key moments. In some fan cultures, throwing or giving a scarf to a player after a match is a gesture of great respect.

**Ultras** — organised groups of the most intense supporters. They stand in the same section of the stadium at every match, lead singing and chanting, create choreographed displays (tifos) with flags and banners, and generally make a lot of noise. Italian and South American ultras are particularly famous. French ultras — at Marseille, Paris, and Lyon in particular — are central to the atmosphere of Ligue 1.

**The Ballon d'Or** — awarded each year by France Football magazine to the best player in the world, voted by journalists. It is the most prestigious individual award in the sport. Messi and Ronaldo have won it a combined 12 times between them.
